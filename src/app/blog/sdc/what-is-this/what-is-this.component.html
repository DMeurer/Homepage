SDC — What is this?

Operating rooms are full of devices that ignore each other. The insufflator doesn't know the HF generator exists. The pump can't tell the monitor it's about to run dry. Every one of them has a screen, a beeper and its own idea of what an alarm looks like, and none of that reaches anything else in the room.

That isn't for lack of trying. It's that "connecting two medical devices" is a much harder problem than it looks, and until recently there was no standard answer to it.

The IEEE 11073 SDC Standard wants to be an Answer to that that Problem. This post is what I wish I'd read when I started.

The problem SDC actually solves

The obvious framing is "medical devices should talk to each other", which makes it sound like a networking problem. It isn't. The networking part is relatively easy, just connected to some organisation problems when it comes to actually using this.

The hard parts are these:

Nobody agrees what a value means. A device sends 42 for example.
Forty-two what? Millilitres per minute? Percent? Pressure? We need a unit.
Where did you measure? What is the meaning of the measurement. A temperature has very different interpretations depending on where you measured it.
What precision is it measured to? When was it measured?

Devices are built by companies who have never met.
A point-to-point integration between two vendors works, but it works only once. I am not talking about self-destruct, I am stating that in the past, two devices communicating required a custom integration and they had to be built with the communication partner in mind.
But its not realistic to have a company write integrations for a ton of products.

Someone has to be responsible. This is the one that makes medical different from many other domains.
Someone has to be responsible. Who is responsible if a device controls another in a wrong way? Who is responsible if communication errors cause a device to misbehave?
In the medical field a error can have catastrophic consequences, that do not have a undo button.
Conformity assessment assumes a single manufacturer who is responsible for the whole product. Cross-vendor networking breaks that assumption, and it is a genuinely unsolved problem rather than an oversight.1

SDC attacks the first two, and the last one a bit. It gives devices a shared vocabulary for describing themselves, and a shared way of finding each other and exchanging data. The third is a regulatory question that a protocol can't fix on its own, but the protocol defines how trust works.

How it works

The Basics - starting at the Tree

An SDC device describes itself in an MDIB — the Medical Device Information Base. It represents the entire device at a point in time, and it has two halves:

  • MdDescription — the structure. What this device is. Which values it has, what units they're in, what may be changed.
  • MdState — the contents. What those values are right now.

The structure is a containment tree, and the levels have fixed meanings:2

MDS is the whole device. A VMD is a functional module — a ventilator might have a gas delivery VMD and a monitoring VMD. Channels group things that belong together, and metrics are the leaves where actual values live.

The point of the fixed hierarchy is that a consumer who has never seen your device can still walk it and find things.
Additionally the standard committee can define a standard set of VMDs and what channels and metrics they should have. This way, two similar devices from different vendors can look the same to a consumer. That it what enables the interoperability that SDC is aiming for.3

There are only five datatypes

Might be a surprise, but the standard currently only requires 5 datatypes. The BICEPS also doesn't let you invent value types.4

Type What it holds
NumericMetric a number, with a unit and a resolution
StringMetric free text
EnumStringMetric one of a fixed list of allowed texts / values
RealTimeSampleArrayMetric a waveform — a stream of samples
DistributionSampleArrayMetric values spread over a range, like a frequency spectrum

There's no floating point number. Floating point arithmetic is a nogo anyways. Numbers are exact decimals instead, so 12.5 is no problem, it just isn't stored as a float. On top of that the NumericMetric supports a unit and a resolution: the smallest step the device can tell apart, for example 0.1.
There's no boolean. But a Enum with only two possible values really is the same anyway.
And there's no binary blob — SDC deliberately doesn't carry audio or video.5 If you want a camera feed, it goes over its own transport and SDC only carries the URL to the stream.

That constraint sounds annoying but mostly isn't. It means every consumer knows how to read every value it will ever encounter. Allowing arbitrary types would break this.

Remote control is a separate object

Here's the part I got wrong at first. I assumed a settable value would have a flag on it — something like writable: true.

It doesn't work like that. If a value can be changed from outside, there's a separate descriptor for the operation, living in the device's SCO, and it points at the metric it targets:

So it's just like a setZoomLevel() method in code.
The Metric might for example have a technical minimum and maximum, but the SCO Object can set its own allowed values or limits to limit what external control can do.

This might feel like overkill but it buys you a lot of freedom.
Each operation carries its own access level and its own permitted range, and the whole thing can be switched off at runtime without changing the device's description. Two operations can target the same metric with different permissions.6

Remote control operations aren't fire-and-forget. You send a request, and the device reports back through a sequence of states — waiting, started, then finished or failed. The device is also allowed to say no and refuse the remote control.7

Alarms - Why are they separate?

Alert are states that are either active or not. Sounds like a boolean.
But alerts are such a common event that it makes sense to have a system just for this with very much flexibility around them.

Alerts for example have specified signals. Alarms are typically perceived in three ways: through visuals, audio or haptics. And a other type just in case.
They can also be latched. Meaning if they only fire for a split second they are not immediately off again, but stay raised until a acknowledgement clears it.8

Finding each other

There's no registry and no broker. Devices announce themselves using multicast when they go online, and consumers can send a probe to ask who's out there. A device that's leaving says so.
A discovery probes and hello messages carrie the device's endpoint and some other information. A consumer can then request the MDIB to look at the device in its full state.9

Security and Trust

I believe its self explanatory why this is important, so lets skip to how and the problems with it.
The entire communication after the discovery is using TLS. But same certificates are also used for trust.10
Basically a communication partner will exchange certificates for encrypted traffic anyways. Based on the Certificate a device can then decide if it want to trust the certificate or not. Additionally a entire CA can be marked trusted, so every certificate signed by the CA is auto-trusted.

But who do you trust?

This may seem simple, but this is the main legal concern. The companies could set the trusted CAs fixed on their own CA and some other companies that pay enough. But now the producer has to make sure all devices there actually can be trusted. This is probably a legal risk no one is willing to take, so this decision will likely land with the hospital.

The problem with this system

It really is a great solution in theory. You have encrypted communication, and fine-grained trust if you want it.
BUT
How do certificates work again? - Ah yes, they are validated against a Certificate Authority (CA) and a Certificate Revocation List (CRL).
So for this to work you need one. Or multiple ones.
This requires internet - something not many medical devices have access to. And for a reason. The more you expose devices to the internet, the more attack vectors you have. And it is used in a pretty critical area.
A solution for this: Well just make it local. Not a bad Idea, but now you not only have to maintain the devices and the devices network, but also a full on CA and CRL and every single Certificate for every device.

So either way, the Hospital IT department will not like it.

Seeing it work

Reading about a containment tree only gets you so far. I built a small desktop tool — the SDC Testing Toolbox — mostly so I could poke at this stuff and watch what happened.
(Be aware this is a testing tool and I had much help from various LLMs. Its not intended for anything other than testing stuff.)

How to use it

You start it twice. Each instance publishes a device and can be a consumer for multiple others.
There are multiple presets to choose from if you want a ready to go starting point. You can also toss your own MDIB file in.
If you dont have a MDIB at hand but know hat a device should look like, you can also start the tool without a template and add a nuch of datasources, alers and actions. It features a export to MDIB, either a clean one or one with toolbox metadata. The toolbox simplifies a lot, for example, there is only one "default channel". These are some shortcuts I took, to keep the tool simple and at least a bit less overwhelming for beginners.

What SDC is not

Worth being clear, because the marketing isn't always:

It is not real-time.
The datatype RealTimeSampleArrayMetric and the validity atribute of a metric suggest this, but it is not a real-time protocol.11

It is not plug-and-play yet
Two devices can discover each other and exchange a well-formed MDIB, but that is about it at the moment. The device specializations are not nearly completed (at the time of writing, end of 2026), and only half of the key purposes are published.12 Pretty bad when you consider the research started in 2005 and the Standard of the family was published in 2016.

None of that makes it uninteresting. It's one of the only attempts to standardize at this scale, and it can work if enough companies start using it.

Where to look next

The standards themselves are paywalled, which is a shame. But OR.NET publishes a good deal openly, there are open source implementations to read, and the model is legible enough that an afternoon with a running device teaches more than a week of specification.

If you want to try it, the SDC Testing Toolbox is a couple of clicks away from a device on your network.
